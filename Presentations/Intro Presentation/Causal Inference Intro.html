A Brief Stint into Causal Inference

Johnson Vo

2026-01-13

What is Causal Inference?

  • Most research questions in research seek to find causal solutions
  • Standard statistical analyses primarily infers associations rather than causality
  • In many cases association does not imply causation
  • RCTs are the gold standard - but are expensive and often not possible
  • Observational data is abundant and inexpensive
  • Treatment and control groups differ in ways that affect the outcomes

Potential Outcome Framework

  • For each unit \(i\), define potential outcomes under each treatment:
  • \(Y_i(1)\): outcome if treated (\(A_i = 1\))
  • \(Y_i(0)\): outcome if untreated (\(A_i = 0\))

We only observe one potential outcome

  • If \(A_i = 1\), we observe \(Y_i(1)\) but \(Y_i(0)\) is counterfactual
  • If \(A_i = 0\), we observe \(Y_i(0)\) but \(Y_i(1)\) is counterfactual

Average Treatment Effect (ATE): \(\text{ATE} = E[Y(1) - Y(0)] = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\)

Key Assumptions Needed

  1. SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption)
  2. Consistency: \(Y = Y(A)\)
  3. Ignorability/Exchangeability: The potential outcomes are independent of treatment assignment given confounders: \(Y(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid X\)
  4. Positivity: \(0 < P(A=1|X) < 1\) and \(0 < P(A=0|X) < 1\)

The Role of Confounding

Confounder X:

  • Affects treatment assignment \(A\)
  • Affects outcome \(Y\)
  • Creates spurious association

Bias: \(\begin{align} &E[Y|A=1] - E[Y|A=0]\\ &= \underbrace{E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]}_{\text{Causal effect}} + \underbrace{\text{Confounding bias}}_{\neq 0} \end{align}\)

  • Under ignorability, the observed distribution of the outcome in treatment arm \(A=a\) equals the distribution of the potential outcome \(Y(a)\)
  • Treatment assignment is randomized!
  • Rules out any confounders

The Core Problem: Unmeasured Confounding

  • Unmeasured confounding violates the strongly ignorable treatment assignment assumption and leads to biased causal effect estimates
  • The Problem: When unmeasured confounder \(U\) exists: \(U\) is unobserved → standard methods produce biased estimates
  • This is an untestable assumption

Paper 1: Spatial Causal Inference (Reich et al.)

Motivating Example: Air Pollution & Birth Weight

Setup:

  • \(Y_{ij}\): Birth weight (baby \(j\) in zip code \(i\))
  • \(A_{ij}\): Air pollution exceeds threshold
  • \(X_{ij}\): Mother’s age, income, known pollutants
  • \(U_i\): Unknown pollutant (unmeasured spatial confounder)

Spatial Challenges Beyond Standard Causal Inference

Three Key Complications:

  1. Spatial Confounding
    • Treatment exhibits spatial patterns
    • Unmeasured confounders have spatial structure
  2. Violation of SUTVA via Interference
    • Treatment at location \(i\) affects outcomes at location \(j\)
    • Violates SUTVA: \(Y_{ij}\) depends on \((A_{ij}, A_{-ij})\)
  3. Spatiotemporal Dependencies
    • Data evolve over space and time
    • Temporal confounding + spatial confounding
    • Complex correlation structures

Methods for Spatial Confounding (Reich et al.)

1. Case-Control Matching

  • Match observations within the same region (outcome based)
  • Ensure cases and controls are comparable with respect to confounders when estimating an exposure–outcome association
  • Differences in the same region eliminate confounder \(U_i\)
  • For continuous outcome: \(Y_{ij} - Y_{il} = (A_{ij} - A_{il})\beta + (X_{ij} - X_{il})\gamma + \epsilon\)

2. Neighborhood Adjustments

  • Remove large-scale spatial trends
  • SAR model: Subtract neighborhood means
  • Spatial smoothing: Joint modeling of treatment & confounder

Methods for Spatial Confounding (continued)

3. Spatial Propensity Score Methods

  • Propensity scores represent the probability of treatment assignment given the covariates including \(U_i\), i.e \(e_{ij} = P(A_{ij}=1|X_{ij}, U_{i})\)

\[\begin{align} Y_{ij} &= A_{ij}\beta + X_{ij}\gamma + U_i + \epsilon_{ij}\\ \text{logit}(e_{ij}) &= X_{ij}\alpha + \phi U_i + V_i \end{align}\]

  • Problem: If \(U_i\) is spatial and unmeasured, people in the same region have similar \(U_i\) but different propensity scores

Reparameterize spatial random effects

  • \(Y_i = A_i\beta + X_i\gamma + u_i + \psi v_i + \epsilon_i\)
  • \(u_i\) = spatial variation in outcome
  • \(\psi v_i\) = shared spatial effect from treatment model \(\text{logit}(e_i) = X_i\alpha + v_i\)

where \(v_i\) is a spatial random effect

  • \(v_i\) captures spatial patterns in treatment
  • Nearby regions have similar \(v_i\)
  • Acts as proxy for unmeasured \(U_i\)
  • Shared spatial effect \(v_i\) adjusts for unmeasured confounding

4. Instrumental Variables

  • Need valid IV \(Z_i\) such that:
    1. \(Z_i\) associated with \(A_i\)
    2. \(Z_i\) not related to \(U_i\)
    3. \(Z_i\) affects \(Y_i\) only through \(A_i\)
  • Confines the treatment variable to the span of the IV (and thus the unmeasured confounder)

Example: Traffic density as IV for air pollution

Spatiotemporal Extensions (Reich et al.)

Temporal dimension adds more information

Difference-in-Differences: - Aim to quantify the treatment effect on the increase in the mean response over time

\[Y_{it} = \beta_1 A_{it} + \beta_2 t + \beta_3 t \cdot A_{it} + X_{it}\gamma + U_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\]

  • \(\beta_3\) = causal effect
  • Controls for time-invariant spatial confounding

Spatiotemporal Extensions (Reich et al.)

Granger Causality:

  • Different framework from potential outcomes
  • Based on predictive reduction
  • \(A\) Granger-causes \(Y\) if: \[\text{Var}(Y_t|H_t) > \text{Var}(Y_t|H_t, A_1, \ldots, A_{t-1})\]
  • Equivalent to Rubin causality under certain conditions

Paper 2: Time-Varying Unmeasured Confounding (Zou et al.)

Longitudinal Data Framework

Data structure: \(\{X_{i1}, A_{i1}, \ldots, X_{iJ}, A_{iJ}, Y_i\}_{i=1}^n\)

  • \(X_j\): Measured confounders at visit \(j\)
  • \(A_j \in \{0,1\}\): Treatment at visit \(j\)
  • \(U_j\): Time-varying unmeasured confounder
  • \(Y\): End-of-study outcome

Sequential Latent Ignorability: \[Y(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A_j \mid \bar{A}_{j-1}, \bar{X}_j, \bar{U}_j\]

Without conditioning on \(U\), we have unidentifiable bias

Zou et al.: Two Bayesian Approaches

1. Latent Variable Approach

Idea: Model \(U\) explicitly with bias parameters

Bias parameters: \(\lambda = \{\alpha_U, \gamma_U, \tau_U, \beta_U\}\)

Prior specification:

  • Uniform priors: \(\beta_U \sim \text{Uniform}(-2, 2)\)
  • Expert knowledge / external data
  • Vague when uncertain

2. Sensitivity Function Approach

Idea: Directly characterize bias without modeling \(U\)

Sensitivity function: \[c(j, a_J, x_J) = E[Y(a)|A_j=a_j, X_j=x_j] \\- E[Y(a)|A_j=1-a_j, \bar{A}_{j-1}=\bar{a}_{j-1}, X_j=x_j]\]

When ignorability holds: \(c(\cdot) = 0\)

Derive bias-corrected outcome

Bayesian Latent Variable Approach: Details

Joint likelihood (marginalizing over \(U\)): \[P(O|\Lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \sum_{u_{i1}=0}^1 \cdots \sum_{u_{iJ}=0}^1 P(Y_i|A_i, X_i, U_i, \beta) \prod_{j=1}^J P(A_{ij}|\cdot, \alpha_j)P(U_{ij}|\cdot, \tau_j)P(X_{ij}|\cdot, \gamma_j)\]

  1. Specify priors for bias parameters (and other parameters)
  2. Run MCMC to get posterior \(f(\Lambda|O)\)
  3. For each posterior draw \(\Lambda^{(s)}\):
    • Predict \(U^{(s)}\) at each visit
    • Compute \(E[Y_i(a)|\Lambda^{(s)}]\) via g-formula
    • Average over subjects
  4. APO posterior: \(E[Y(a)|O] = \int E[Y(a)|\Lambda]f(\Lambda|O)d\Lambda\)

Sensitivity Function Approach: Details

Bias decomposition: \[E[Y(a)] = E[Y|A=a] - \underbrace{\int \sum_{j=1}^J c(j,a_J,x_J)P(1-a_j|\bar{a}_{j-1}, x_j) \prod P(x_j|\cdot)dx}_{\text{Bias from unmeasured confounding}}\]

Implementation with Bayesian MSM:

  1. Estimate sensitivity function (several options):

    • Subject-matter expertise
    • Bounded by outcome range
    • From residual variance of conditional model

  2. Compute bias-corrected outcome:

  3. Bayesian MSM on \(Y^{SF}\) via importance sampling & Bayesian bootstrap

Zou et al.: Simulation Results

Scenarios tested:

Key Findings:

Method Time-varying \(U\) Multiple \(U\)s
MSM (no adjustment) High bias High bias
BSA time-invariant \(U\) Moderate bias Poor
BSA time-varying \(U\) Good Good
Sensitivity Function Excellent* Excellent*

*Used true sensitivity function in simulations

Recommendations:

✓ Use BSA time-varying \(U\) for longitudinal data

✓ Sensitivity function can handle complex confounding

✗ Avoid BSA time-invariant when confounding varies over time

Practical steps:

  1. Draw DAG with suspected \(U\)
  2. Justify ignorability or specify bias
  3. Conduct sensitivity analysis
  4. Report range of estimates under plausible scenarios

Open Questions & Future Directions

From Reich et al.:

  • Relax parametric assumptions (non-parametric spatial methods)
  • Quantile/extreme value spatial causal inference
  • Spatially varying treatment effects
  • Optimal spatial treatment allocation
  • Change of support problems

From Zou et al.:

  • Model misspecification robustness
  • Multiple/complex temporal patterns of \(U\)
  • Flexible estimation (BART, etc.)
  • Repeated outcomes (not just end-of-study)
  • Time-to-event outcomes
