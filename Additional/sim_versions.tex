\section{Motivation}

The simulation reflects the two-level structure of the application: area-level count
health outcomes for participants nested within Forward Sortation Areas (FSA or the first
three characters of a Canadian postal code), with area-level exposure assigned from a
spatiotemporal prediction model.

\section{Notation}

\begin{itemize}
\item $i = 1, \ldots, I$: area index (FSAs)
\item $A_i$: latent true area-level PM$_{2.5}$ exposure, unobserved
\item $Z_i$: observed error-prone surrogate from a spatiotemporal prediction model
(e.g. CanOSSEM)
\item $\mathbf{X}_i = (X_{i1}, \ldots, X_{i4})^\top$: area-level confounders affecting
both exposure and outcome
\item $D_i$: area-level calibration covariate explaining prediction error magnitude in
$Z_i$ (e.g. distance to nearest monitoring station, area deprivation)
\item $Y_i$: area-level count health outcome (number of incident depression cases)
\item $N_i$: area population size, used as Poisson offset
\item $\phi_i$: spatial random effect on the latent exposure surface, following the
CAR prior in equation (1)
\item $\mathbf{W}$: $I \times I$ binary adjacency matrix ($W_{ij} = 1$ if areas $i$ and
$j$ share a border)
\item $\mathbf{D}_W = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{1})$: diagonal degree matrix
(number of neighbours per area)
\item $\rho_A \in [0, 1)$: spatial correlation in the exposure CAR prior
\item $\sigma_\phi^2$: marginal variance of the spatial random effect $\phi_i$
\end{itemize}

As in the thesis proposal, $\mathbf{X}_i$ controls confounding while $D_i$ indexes the
measurement error mechanism. $D_i$ may overlap with $\mathbf{X}_i$ but is conceptually
distinct.

\section{Key Assumptions}

The simulation satisfies the following identification assumptions from the thesis proposal.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-differential surrogate:} $Y_i \perp Z_i \mid A_i, \mathbf{X}_i, D_i$.
Once we condition on the true exposure and covariates, the surrogate carries no additional
information about the outcome. Satisfied by construction since $Y_i$ is generated from
$A_i$ only and $Z_i$ never appears in the outcome equation.

\item \textbf{Transportability:} The error relationship is assumed transportable to the
target population. When direct calibration data are unavailable, inference about $A_i$ is
more model-dependent; this is examined in sensitivity scenario S2.
\end{itemize}

\section{Base Model}
\label{sec:base}

\subsection{Spatial Structure and CAR Prior}

Areas are arranged on a $\sqrt{I} \times \sqrt{I}$ synthetic grid with rook contiguity
(shared edges only). For this simulation the base value is $I = 400$ (a $20 \times 20$ grid); $I = 196$ and $I = 900$ are used as smaller and larger cases.

Following thesis proposal Section 4.3, spatial dependence enters only through the
latent exposure model via $\phi_i$. The spatial random effect follows the Conditional
Autoregressive (CAR) prior:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\phi} \mid \rho_A, \sigma_\phi^2 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\;
\sigma_\phi^2(\mathbf{D}_W - \rho_A\mathbf{W})^{-1}\right). \label{eq:car}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\item $\phi_i$ is a spatially structured random effect: neighbouring areas have similar
$\phi_i$ values, inducing correlation in their true exposures $A_i$
\item The precision matrix $(\mathbf{D}_W - \rho_A\mathbf{W})$ is sparse with adjacent
pairs having off-diagonal entry $-\rho_A$ and non-adjacent pairs having zero
\item $\rho_A = 0$: areas are spatially independent;
$\rho_A \to 1$: strong smoothing across neighbours
\item Spatial correlation in the outcome is induced indirectly through $A_i$; no
separate outcome random effect is added
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Covariates and Population Sizes}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Area confounders:} $X_{i1}, X_{i3}, X_{i4} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$;
$X_{i2} = (G - 2)/\sqrt{2}$ with $G \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 1)$ is right-skewed (skewness $1.41$). All four have
mean 0 and variance 1, so the exposure-variance bookkeeping below is unchanged. The skewed one is the
confounder with the smallest outcome coefficient, which limits how much its heavy tail inflates the gap
between a dataset's covariate-averaged ERF and the population value
\item \textbf{Population sizes:} $\log(N_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(8.5, 1.2^2)$, giving
median $\approx 4{,}900$, 5th percentile $\approx 600$, 95th percentile $\approx 40{,}000$.
Motivated by Statistics Canada 2021 Census: Ontario has $\approx 500$ FSAs with total
population 14,223,942, ranging from dense urban FSAs in Toronto to sparse rural FSAs in
Northern Ontario.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration Covariate $D_i$}

$D_i$ captures area characteristics that determine prediction error in $Z_i$.

\paragraph{Base model: scalar $D_i$.}
We generate a raw covariate $D_i^* \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Gamma}(2, 1)$
(mean 2, variance 2) representing distance to the nearest monitoring station, then
standardise:
\[
D_i = \frac{D_i^* - 2}{\sqrt{2}},
\]
so that $D_i$ has mean 0 and variance 1. Standardisation prevents numerical blow-up in
the exponential error variance. The interpretation of $\eta_1$
demonstrates that a one standard deviation increase in monitor distance multiplies the error
variance by $\exp(\eta_1)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Distance is strictly positive and right-skewed and hence a Gamma distribution naturally showcases this.
Most Ontario FSAs are moderately close to one of the 34 monitoring stations, but some rural
areas are very far.

\item \textbf{Motivation:} Gryparis et al. (2009) and Szpiro et al. (2011)
show that prediction error variance is primarily determined by distance to the
monitor network. MELONS (Evangelopoulos et al., 2026) identified distance, deprivation,
and housing type as empirical determinants of PM$_{2.5}$ measurement error in London, UK
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Extension: vector $D_i$.}
In a full implementation $D_i$ would be a vector with components such as:
$D_{i1} \sim \text{Gamma}(2,1)$ (distance to monitor, standardised),
$D_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (income and employment indicators as a standardised
score), $D_{i3} \sim \text{Beta}(2,2)$ (proportion detached housing).
The error model would be $\omega^2(\mathbf{D}_i) = \exp(\eta_0 + \eta_1 D_{i1} +
\eta_2 D_{i3})$. Left as a future extension.

\subsection{Latent True Exposure}

The latent true exposure is generated from:
\begin{equation}
A_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i, \phi_i \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(10 - 0.75X_{i1} + 0.75X_{i2} -
0.75X_{i3} + 0.75X_{i4} + \phi_i,\ 2.25\right), \label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_A = 1.5$, where $\phi_i$ is the spatial random effect following the CAR prior
defined in equation~\eqref{eq:car} with parameters $(\rho_A, \sigma_\phi^2)$. The spatial
random effect $\phi_i$ induces correlation in $A_i$ across neighbouring areas: areas with
$\rho_A$ close to 1 have exposures that vary smoothly across the grid, reflecting a
regional wildfire plume, while $\rho_A = 0$ gives spatially independent exposures.
The exposure is not truncated: with a mean of 10 and a total SD of about 2.4, the Normal model puts
essentially no mass below zero (no negative $A_i$ in 60 test datasets).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mean = 10 $\mu$g/m$^3$:} A cumulative wildfire-smoke exposure level, chosen high enough
relative to the spread that $A_i$ and $Z_i$ are essentially never negative, so no truncation or
censoring is needed (about 5 to 15 $\mu$g/m$^3$ covers the 1st to 99th percentile)
\item \textbf{$\sigma_A = 1.5$:} Residual SD; with the covariate and spatial components the total SD of $A_i$
is about 2.4
\item \textbf{Covariate coefficients $\pm 0.75$:} The signs, set against the outcome coefficients listed under the
outcome model below, create real confounding: $\sum_k \gamma_k \beta_k = 0.375$, so an
analysis that ignores the confounders has a log-linear exposure slope biased by about $0.07$ against a true
slope of $0.10$ (the confounders' combined effect on the outcome has correlation about $0.2$ with $A_i$).
With the sign pattern $(+,-,-,+)$ used by Josey et al.\ (2023) this sum is exactly zero, so the confounding
there operates only through the exposure--covariate interaction
\item \textbf{Note on skewness:} Ontario PM$_{2.5}$ is heavily right-skewed (skewness
$\approx 9.2$, 99th percentile $\approx 26.4$, extreme values $> 200$ $\mu$g/m$^3$
during severe wildfire events). The Normal captures typical days but not extreme events.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Choosing $\sigma_\phi^2$ via $R^2$ decomposition.}
With the exposure model now defined, we can determine $\sigma_\phi^2$ by targeting a
meaningful proportion of the total variance in $A_i$. The total variance decomposes into
three sources: the covariate contribution $\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_i\text{ terms}) =
4 \times 0.75^2 = 2.25$, the spatial contribution $\text{Var}(\phi_i)$, and the residual
$\sigma_A^2 = 2.25$. Under the CAR prior the average marginal variance of $\phi_i$ is
$\sigma_\phi^2 \, \bar{q}$, where $\bar{q} = \frac{1}{I}\,\text{tr}\{(\mathbf{D}_W -
\rho_A\mathbf{W})^{-1}\}$ is computed exactly from the grid. (The cruder approximation
$\bar{q} \approx 1/\bar{d}$ with $\bar{d} \approx 3.6$ neighbours badly understates it at
$\rho_A = 0.9$: a target of $0.30$ gave a realised $R^2_\phi$ of about $0.50$.) The
proportion of variance explained by the spatial random effect is:
\[
R^2_\phi = \frac{\sigma_\phi^2 \bar{q}}{\text{Var}(A_i)} = \frac{\sigma_\phi^2 \bar{q}}{9 + \sigma_\phi^2 \bar{q} + 9}.
\]
Setting a target $R^2_\phi$ and solving gives $\sigma_\phi^2 = R^2_\phi \times 18 /
\{(1 - R^2_\phi)\,\bar{q}\}$. At the previously used value of $\sigma_\phi^2 = 0.3$, the
implied $R^2_\phi \approx 0.5\%$ --- essentially no spatial contribution. We therefore
parameterise through the target $R^2_\phi$, with base value $0.20$, and compute the
implied $\sigma_\phi^2$ accordingly; the realised share is about $0.20$.

\subsection{Measurement Error Model}

Two parameterisations of the measurement error model are considered. Both generate $Z_i$
as a function of the true exposure $A_i$ and calibration covariate $D_i$, and both
satisfy the non-differential assumption by construction since $Z_i$ does not appear in
the outcome equation.

In both versions the measurement errors $\varepsilon_i = Z_i - \mathbb{E}[Z_i \mid A_i, D_i]$
are assumed conditionally independent across areas given $(A_i, D_i)$. This is a
simplifying assumption: Gryparis et al.\ (2009) show that prediction errors from spatial
models are spatially correlated because nearby areas share the same monitoring station
data when their surrogates are generated. Incorporating spatially correlated errors is
listed as a potential extension.

\paragraph{Version A: Classical error with Di-dependent mean (misspecification scenario).}

Following Wu et al.\ (2019), $D_i$ enters through the mean of the surrogate,
introducing systematic directional bias:
\begin{equation}
Z_i \mid A_i, D_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta_{Z1} A_i + \beta_{Z2} D_i,\ \sigma_Z^2).
\label{eq:errorA}
\end{equation}
This model is not fully identifiable when $A_i$ is latent: $\beta_{Z1}$ and $\sigma_Z^2$
cannot be separately recovered from the marginal distribution of $Z_i$ without validation
data.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\beta_{Z1}$}: attenuation. $\beta_{Z1} = 1$ centres the surrogate on the
truth; $\beta_{Z1} < 1$ attenuates high exposures. Wu et al.\ (2019) use $\beta_1 = 0.8$
as their default.
\item \textbf{$\beta_{Z2}$}: directional bias from $D_i$. When $\beta_{Z2} > 0$ the
surrogate overestimates true exposure in high-$D_i$ areas (far from monitors), consistent
with the MELONS finding of systematic overestimation in deprived and remote areas. When
$\beta_{Z2} = 0$ this reduces to standard classical error.
\item \textbf{$\sigma_Z^2$}: fixed homoscedastic noise. Heterogeneity across areas is
captured entirely through the mean shift $\beta_{Z2} D_i$.
\end{itemize}

Wu et al.\ (2019) write this in the regression calibration direction as
$E(X \mid W, D) = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 W + \gamma_2^\top D$.

\paragraph{Version B: Classical error with Di-dependent variance (primary model).}

$D_i$ enters through the variance of the surrogate, relaxing the homoscedasticity
assumption of Josey et al.\ (2023) Assumption 2:
\begin{equation}
Z_i \mid A_i, D_i \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\exp(\beta_1 D_i)\,A_i,\ \sigma_Z^2(D_i)\big), \qquad
\sigma_Z^2(D_i) = \exp(\eta_0 + \eta_1 D_i). \label{eq:errorB}
\end{equation}
The surrogate is not truncated; with $\eta_0 = -0.5$ a negative $Z_i$ is very rare (at most a couple of
areas in occasional datasets) and is harmless because the Normal error model is exact.

\begin{itemize}
\item With $\eta_0 = -0.5$ the base value $\eta_1 = 0.5$ keeps the error SD moderate (median about
0.7, 99th percentile about 1.8, typical maximum about 2.7). At $\eta_1 = 1$ the right tail of $D_i$ would give a handful
of areas a far larger error SD, and these areas would carry most of the total error variance.
\item The calibration slope $\exp(\beta_1 D_i)$ is close to 1 on average ($\beta_1 = -0.1$ gives a
mean slope of about 1.0) but falls below 1 in areas far from monitors (roughly 0.7 to 0.8 for $D_i > 1.5$),
so the surrogate is attenuated and also noisier in high-$D_i$ areas. The log link keeps the slope
positive despite the long right tail of $D_i$
\item $\eta_1 = \beta_1 = 0$ recovers Josey's homoscedastic, unattenuated Assumption 2 exactly
\item $\exp(\eta_0 + \eta_1 D_i)$ ensures positivity and implies a log-linear
(proportional) relationship: a unit increase in $D_i$ multiplies the error variance by
$\exp(\eta_1)$, natural for distance-based quantities (Gryparis et al., 2009)
\item Unlike Version A, there is no directional shift in the mean; the departures from classical
error are an attenuation and a noise magnitude that both depend on $D_i$
\item This version is identifiable: the marginal variance of $Z_i$ given
$(\mathbf{X}_i, D_i)$ is $\exp(2\beta_1 D_i)\,(\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_\phi^2 \bar{q}) + \exp(\eta_0 + \eta_1 D_i)$,
allowing estimation of $(\eta_0, \eta_1)$ without observing $A_i$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Outcome Model}

The area-level count outcome follows a Poisson model with a log-linear predictor and
population offset, matching Josey et al. (2023):
\begin{equation}
Y_i \mid A_i, \mathbf{X}_i, N_i \sim \text{Poisson}(N_i \exp(\eta_i)),
\label{eq:outcome}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\eta_i = -3 + f(A_i) - 0.5X_{i1} - 0.25X_{i2} + 0.25X_{i3} + 0.5X_{i4}.
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Population offset $\log(N_i)$:} $N_i$ scales the expected count so that
$\exp(\eta_i)$ is the event rate per person. A larger area will have more events simply
because it has more people; the offset removes this confound. In R this enters as an
additive term $\log(N_i)$ in the linear predictor or via the \texttt{offset} argument.
\item \textbf{Intercept $-3$:} $\exp(-3) \approx 5\%$ baseline event rate, representing
baseline depression incidence in the CLSA population (Raina et al., 2019)
\item \textbf{Confounder coefficients $\pm(0.5, 0.25)$:} Follow Josey et al. (2023)
\item \textbf{ERF $f(A_i)$:} Exposure-response function specified in sensitivity
scenario S4; three functional forms are evaluated
\item \textbf{No spatial random effect:} Consistent with thesis proposal Section 4.3;
spatial correlation in outcomes is induced indirectly through the spatially correlated
$A_i$ surface
\end{itemize}

$Z_i$ does not appear in equation~\eqref{eq:outcome}, satisfying the non-differential
assumption by construction.

\subsection{Causal Estimand}

Following Josey et al.\ (2023), the target estimand is the marginal exposure-response
function (ERF):
\[
\theta(a) \equiv \mathbb{E}[Y_i(a)],
\]
where $Y_i(a)$ is the potential outcome that would occur in area $i$ if, possibly contrary
to what is observed, it had received exposure level $A_i = a$. The causal contrast
between two exposure levels is $\Delta(a_1, a_0) = \theta(a_1) - \theta(a_0)$. Under
the identifying assumptions in Section~3, the ERF is identified by the g-formula:
\[
\theta(a) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid A_i = a, \mathbf{X}_i]\right].
\]

\begin{itemize}
\item The true $\theta(a)$ is computed by Monte Carlo using each replicate's own
simulated covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$ (fresh per replicate, following Josey et al.\ 2023
\texttt{predict\_example}), rather than a separate fixed external population. This lets
the benchmark truth reflect the sampling variability of the covariate distribution itself
alongside the estimators being evaluated
\item \textbf{Evaluation grid:} the integers 5 to 15 ($11$ values), covering the bulk of the
$A_i$ distribution (1st to 99th percentile about 4.6 to 15.4)
\item \textbf{Primary causal contrast: extreme vs typical exposure:}
$\Delta(Q_{99}, Q_{50})$ where $Q_{99}$ and $Q_{50}$ are the 99th and 50th percentiles
of the simulated $A_i$ distribution. For the base model these correspond
approximately to $Q_{50} \approx 10$ and $Q_{99} \approx 15.4$, representing
the increase in expected depression case count on a severe wildfire day relative to a
typical day.
The percentiles are computed from the simulated $A_i$ values in each replicate so that
the contrast automatically adapts to the exposure distribution under each scenario.
\item \textbf{Secondary contrast: IQR:} $\Delta(Q_{75}, Q_{25})$ following standard
practice in air pollution epidemiology
\end{itemize}

\section{Sensitivity Scenarios}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\paragraph{$D_i$ distribution.}
\textbf{Gamma(2, 0.5)}: mean 4, more heterogeneity between areas.
\textbf{Exponential(1)}: monotone decreasing, consistent with Southern Ontario station
concentration.
\textbf{Empirical}: actual FSA-to-NAPS distances from Statistics Canada shapefiles.

\paragraph{Error model alternatives.}
\textbf{Version A vs Version B}: Data generated under Version A (directional bias,
$\beta_{Z2} \in \{0, 1, 2\}$) but analysed with a Version B error model. Quantifies
performance degradation when the true error has directional bias that the proposed method
does not model. $\beta_{Z2} = 0$ provides an internal consistency check.

\paragraph{Spatial correlation.}
\textbf{Scenario B ($\rho_A = 0$)}: spatially independent exposures. Directly comparable
to Josey et al. (2023) which sets $\phi_i = 0$ for all areas.
\textbf{Scenario A ($\rho_A = 0.9$)}: high spatial smoothing, reflecting a sustained
regional wildfire event where nearby FSAs share similar smoke concentrations.

\paragraph{Exposure-response functional forms.}
Three forms spanning simple to complex, crossed with spatial scenarios to give six
main simulation cells.

\textbf{Linear}: $f(A_i) = 0.10(A_i - 10)$.
Monotone increasing with no curvature.

\textbf{Quadratic}: $f(A_i) = 0.30(A_i - 10) - 0.05(A_i - 10)^2$.
Concave shape that rises then declines within the evaluation window (vertex at $A_i=13$).

\textbf{Nonlinear (up--down--up)}: $f(A_i, X_{i1}) = 0.12(A_i - 10) + 0.55\big[\sin\!\big(2\pi(A_i - 6.25)/9\big) - 0.5\big] - 0.25\tanh\!\big((A_i - 10)/4\big)X_{i1}$, centred so that $f(10, \cdot) = 0$ like the other forms.
A linear trend plus one sine wave: the curve rises to a local maximum near $A_i = 9$, falls to a
local minimum near $A_i = 12.5$, and then rises again through the end of the window. It is not
captured by a linear or quadratic form, and it grows only linearly beyond the window (exposure
rarely exceeds 17), unlike a cubic. The bounded interaction with $X_{i1}$ (at most $0.25$ per SD)
makes the exposure effect depend on $X_{i1}$, in the spirit of Josey et al.\ (2023) but without their
unbounded $(A_i - 10)X_{i1}$ term; the marginal ERF is then a genuine average of different
conditional curves.

\paragraph{Log-normal exposure.}
$\log(A_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(1.68, 0.54)$ matching Ontario moments: median 5.4, mean 7,
SD 5.9. Captures extreme wildfire events (99th percentile $\approx 26$ $\mu$g/m$^3$)
that the Normal cannot represent.

\section{Performance Metrics}

For each scenario and method (naive, oracle, proposed, regression calibration):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bias}: $\hat{\theta}(a) - \theta(a)$ averaged over evaluation points and
replicates
\item \textbf{RMSE}: $\sqrt{R^{-1}\sum_r (\hat{\theta}_r(a) - \theta(a))^2}$
\item \textbf{Coverage}: fraction of 95\% credible intervals containing $\theta(a)$
\item \textbf{Interval width}: mean width of 95\% credible intervals
\item \textbf{Causal contrast bias}: $\hat{\Delta}(Q_{99}, Q_{50}) - \Delta(Q_{99},
Q_{50})$ and $\hat{\Delta}(Q_{75}, Q_{25}) - \Delta(Q_{75}, Q_{25})$, where quantiles
are computed from the simulated $A_i$ distribution in each replicate
\end{itemize}

\section{Potential Extensions}
\label{sec:extensions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Spatially correlated measurement errors:} The current model assumes
conditional independence of measurement errors $\varepsilon_i = Z_i -
\mathbb{E}[Z_i \mid A_i, D_i]$ across areas which is a simplifying assumption that
Gryparis et al.\ (2009) show is violated in practice: prediction errors from spatial
models are correlated across nearby areas because those predictions are generated using
the same monitoring station data. A natural extension is to give the error term its own
spatial covariance structure, either by making $D_i$ spatially structured via a second
CAR prior (so that nearby areas have correlated error variances), or by adding an
explicit spatially correlated residual $\psi_i \sim \text{CAR}(\rho_\varepsilon,
\sigma_\varepsilon^2)$ to the error model.

\item \textbf{Individual-level binary outcome:} Replacing the area-level Poisson count
with individual-level Bernoulli outcomes $Y_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_{ij})$ and
individual covariates $L_{ij}$, matching the CLSA data structure. The Bayesian bootstrap
would draw Dirichlet weights over all $N = \sum_i n_i$ individuals rather than over areas.

\item \textbf{Spatial random effect on outcome ($\nu_i$)}: A second CAR prior capturing
residual spatial clustering in health outcomes (Englert et al., 2025).

\item \textbf{Negative binomial outcome:} The Poisson model assumes
$\text{Var}(Y_i) = \mathbb{E}[Y_i]$, which is often violated in practice when
area-level counts are overdispersed due to unmeasured heterogeneity across FSAs. A
negative binomial outcome relaxes this by introducing a dispersion parameter $\xi > 0$:
\begin{equation*}
Y_i \mid A_i, \mathbf{X}_i, N_i \sim \text{NegBin}(\mu_i, \xi), \qquad
\mu_i = N_i \exp(\eta_i),
\end{equation*}
As $\xi \to \infty$ this recovers the Poisson; small $\xi$ gives strong overdispersion.
The dispersion parameter $\xi = 1$ is a natural starting point and is the value used by
Englert et al.\ (2025) in their simulation. This extension tests whether the proposed
method remains well-calibrated when the outcome model is more variable than the base
Poisson.

\item \textbf{Zero-inflated outcome}: For rare outcomes or small populations where
structural zeros arise.

\item \textbf{Log-normal exposure model:} The base model specifies $A_i$ on the original
scale as a Normal with a floor at zero. An alternative motivated by the right-skewed
nature of PM$_{2.5}$ concentrations and by the suggestion that $\log(A_i)$ should be
regressed on covariates is to model the log-exposure directly:
\begin{equation*}
\log(A_i) \mid \mathbf{X}_i, \phi_i \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 X_{i1}
+ \gamma_2 X_{i2} + \gamma_3 X_{i3} + \gamma_4 X_{i4} + \phi_i,\
\sigma_{\log A}^2\right),
\end{equation*}
so that $A_i = \exp(\log A_i) > 0$ always. The parameters are matched to Ontario
PM$_{2.5}$ empirical moments: setting $\gamma_0 = 1.68$ and $\sigma_{\log A} = 0.73$
gives $\mathbb{E}[A_i] \approx 7$ $\mu$g/m$^3$ and $\text{SD}(A_i) \approx 5.9$
$\mu$g/m$^3$, matching the provincial monitoring data. The error model in Version B
would then operate on the log scale as $\log(Z_i) \mid \log(A_i), D_i \sim
\mathcal{N}(\log(A_i), \exp(\eta_0 + \eta_1 D_i))$, giving multiplicative error on the
original scale.
\end{itemize}
